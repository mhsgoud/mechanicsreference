Interactive mechanics · pin joints, axial forces

A bar resists extension. A truss makes that useful.

Rotate one element to discover its directional stiffness, then combine two elements to support a load. Every value below follows the small-displacement, linear-elastic truss equations.

01 / Project displacement onto the bar

Node A is fixed. Node B is given a horizontal displacement of 1 mm. Rotate the bar and watch the axial extension change.

KBB = (EA/L) n ⊗ n. The eigenvalues are EA/L along the bar and zero perpendicular to it.

Try 90°: horizontal motion has zero first-order axial extension. An unstressed truss bar has no linear transverse stiffness. Finite transverse motion can stretch it at second order; bending resistance needs a beam model.

02 / Build stiffness from geometry

Both base nodes are fixed in x and y. A vertical load acts on the free apex. All three joints are pins; each straight member carries only axial force.

Lower the apex: vertical stiffness approaches zero and member forces grow for the same nonzero load. Reverse P to switch compression to tension. Compression is allowed in this ideal truss model; member buckling, yielding, joint rigidity, and geometric nonlinearity are not included. The flat configuration is a linear mechanism, even at zero load.
