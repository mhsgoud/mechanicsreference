Basic equations

These are the starting identities of the encyclopedia. Continuum mechanics writes them as fields; FEM and numerical methods approximate them on a mesh.

Kinematics

Deformation gradient

F = ∂x / ∂X

Maps a material line element in the reference placement X to the current placement x. All finite-strain measures are built from F.

Kinematics

Green–Lagrange strain

E = ½ (Fᵀ F − I)

Finite strain on the reference configuration. For small displacement gradients it reduces to the infinitesimal strain tensor.

Kinematics

Infinitesimal strain

ε = ½ (∇u + (∇u)ᵀ)

Symmetric gradient of the displacement u. The working strain measure in linear solid mechanics and linear FEM.

Stress

Cauchy traction

t = σ n

Force per current area on a surface with outward normal n. Cauchy stress σ is the primary stress in the current configuration.

Balance

Conservation of mass

∂ρ/∂t + ∇ · (ρ v) = 0

Local mass balance. For incompressible flow or isochoric plasticity this reduces to ∇ · v = 0.

Balance

Linear momentum

∇ · σ + ρ b = ρ a

Cauchy’s first law: divergence of stress plus body force equals mass density times acceleration. At rest, ∇ · σ + ρ b = 0.

Balance

Angular momentum

σ = σᵀ

In the absence of couple stresses, Cauchy stress is symmetric. This is why constitutive models are written for the six independent components of σ.

Constitutive

Hooke’s law (isotropic)

σ = λ tr(ε) I + 2μ ε

Linear isotropic elasticity. Lamé parameters λ, μ (shear modulus). Equivalent form: σ = ℂ : ε.

Weak form

Principle of virtual work

∫ σ : ∇δu dV = ∫ t · δu dA + ∫ ρ b · δu dV

Weak statement of momentum. The finite element method replaces u and δu with shape-function expansions on a mesh.

Finite elements

Discrete linear system

K a = f

After interpolation uʰ = N a and assembly, linear elasticity becomes a stiffness system: unknown nodal degrees of freedom a, load vector f.

Numerical methods

Newton–Raphson

KT Δu = −R(u)

Nonlinear residuals R(u) = 0 are solved by repeated linearizations. KT is the consistent tangent (Jacobian) of the residual.

Fluids

Navier–Stokes (incompressible)

ρ (∂v/∂t + v · ∇v) = −∇p + μ ∇²v + ρ b

Newtonian fluid with viscosity μ, together with ∇ · v = 0. Same continuum momentum law, different constitutive response.

What this encyclopedia covers

After the basic equations, three subjects sit on one pipeline. Continuum mechanics writes the field problem. The finite element method turns it into a discrete system. Numerical methods assemble, integrate, and solve that system.

Continuum mechanics

Continuum mechanics treats solids and fluids as continuous media rather than collections of atoms. The same structure appears in both: kinematics (how deformation is measured), conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, and a constitutive model that closes the system.

In solids, a body can carry shear at rest. Strain, stress, elasticity, plasticity, and finite-deformation measures such as Green and logarithmic strain belong here. In fluids, shear is resisted only while the material deforms; Newton’s law of viscosity and the Navier–Stokes equations are the Newtonian case.

  • Kinematics: small strain vs finite strain; deformation gradient
  • Balance laws: mass, linear momentum, angular momentum, energy
  • Constitutive models for solids and fluids
  • Boundary-value problems: equilibrium, contact, flow, and coupled physics

Finite element method

The finite element method (FEM) approximates a continuum problem on a mesh. The domain is split into elements, unknown fields are interpolated with shape functions, and the strong form is replaced by a weak integral statement—virtual work or a Galerkin projection.

That step produces element matrices, which assemble into a global residual (and, for Newton methods, a tangent stiffness). Element choice, mesh quality, locking, hourglassing, and boundary conditions decide whether the discrete problem is a faithful model of the continuum one.

  • Weak form, Galerkin method, and isoparametric mapping
  • Element families: tets, hexes, mixed, high-order, and unfitted methods
  • Mesh generation, quadrature, and global assembly
  • Nonlinear FEM, contact, dynamics, and condensation

Numerical methods

Once the continuum problem is discrete, the remaining work is numerical: integrating weak forms, solving linear and nonlinear algebraic systems, and controlling error. Gauss quadrature evaluates element integrals. Newton–Raphson linearizes nonlinear residuals. Direct and iterative solvers, with preconditioners, invert or reduce the resulting sparse matrices.

The same toolkit appears in time integration (implicit vs explicit), eigenvalue problems for buckling, and high-performance implementations on CPUs and GPUs. Linear algebra—sparse formats, fill-reducing orderings, and libraries such as BLAS, MKL, and cuSPARSE—is part of the method, not an afterthought.

  • Numerical integration and Gauss points
  • Newton–Raphson, consistent tangents, and convergence tests
  • Linear solvers, preconditioning, and sparse formats
  • Time stepping, stability, and HPC for large systems

The hub follows that order: continuum fundamentals first, then finite elements and numerical methods, then applications (fracture, composites, soils, heat, batteries) and the linear-algebra tools used to compute them. Search the hub for a term such as Green strain, locking, Gauss, or Schur.

Frequently asked questions

Short answers for the three subjects this site is built around.

Where should I start?

Start with the basic equations: deformation gradient, strain, Cauchy stress, conservation of mass and momentum, Hooke’s law, virtual work, and the discrete system K a = f. The hub then expands each identity into kinematics, constitutive models, FEM, and solvers.

What is continuum mechanics?

Continuum mechanics models solids and fluids as continuous media. It combines kinematics, conservation of mass, momentum, and energy, and constitutive laws that relate stress to deformation or deformation rate. This reference starts there, before discretization.

What is the finite element method?

The finite element method (FEM) approximates a continuum problem by dividing the domain into elements, introducing shape functions, and replacing the strong form with a weak integral statement. The result is a discrete algebraic system whose solution approximates the continuum fields.

What numerical methods are covered?

The hub covers Gauss quadrature, assembly, linear and nonlinear solvers, Newton–Raphson iteration, static condensation, time integration, sparse linear algebra, and the libraries used to compute large finite element systems.

Who is this reference for?

Students, researchers, and engineers who need a compact map of continuum mechanics, FEM, and the numerical methods used to solve them—not a substitute for a textbook, but a place to look up the pipeline and the usual failure modes.
